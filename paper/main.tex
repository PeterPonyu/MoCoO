\begin{abstract}
Characterising cellular differentiation from single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) requires representations that capture both discrete cell-type identity and continuous developmental trajectories. We present \MoCoO, a modular framework integrating a Variational Autoencoder (VAE), Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODE), and Momentum Contrast (MoCo), complemented by an optional Phase-2 Flow Matching (FM) refinement step. Through a systematic six-configuration ablation across 20 scRNA-seq datasets evaluated with a proposed six-metric suite covering clustering geometry (ASW, DAV) and embedding quality (DRE, LSE, DREX, LSEX), we demonstrate two central findings. First, the ODE+MoCo combination is the core architectural synergy: VAE+ODE+MoCo achieves four of six top-two finishes among base configurations, including the best ASW ($0.225$) and DAV ($1.478$), plus second-best DRE ($0.640$) and LSE ($0.335$). Second, FM refinement systematically improves latent-space embedding quality---DREX in 92\% and DRE in 88\% of 120 dataset--configuration pairs ($\Delta$DREX$=+0.030$, $\Delta$DRE$=+0.023$). Combined, VAE+ODE+MoCo+FM ranks top-two on five of six metrics across all 12 configurations. ODE smooths the latent manifold along developmental trajectories; MoCo sharpens cluster geometry; FM recovers and amplifies embedding quality post-hoc. Downstream validation confirms that MoCoO latent spaces support annotation transfer, uncertainty quantification, differential expression, and branching detection. Pseudotime predictions correlate significantly with canonical marker genes across all five core developmental systems. We publicly release the MoCoO Python package (\texttt{pip install mocoo}) and full benchmark suite.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%% ============================================================

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has transformed our understanding of cellular heterogeneity by enabling transcriptome-wide profiling at single-cell resolution~\cite{tang2009mrna}. A fundamental analysis task is to learn low-dimensional representations that faithfully capture both \textit{discrete} cell-type identity and \textit{continuous} developmental dynamics such as differentiation trajectories~\cite{kiselev2019challenges,trapnell2014dynamics}. These representations underpin downstream tasks including clustering, pseudotime inference, RNA velocity estimation, and differential expression analysis~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}.

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) have become the de facto standard for scRNA-seq dimensionality reduction, with methods such as scVI~\cite{lopez2018deep} and scVAE~\cite{gronbech2020scvae} demonstrating strong performance through count-based likelihood models (negative binomial, ZINB). However, standard VAE latent spaces tend to be over-smoothed, conflating nearby cell states. Two complementary strategies address this limitation.

First, Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs)~\cite{chen2018neural} provide a principled framework for modelling continuous-time dynamics in latent space. By parameterising the latent derivative with a neural network and integrating via an ODE solver, these models learn smooth trajectories without discrete time-step assumptions. Latent ODE models~\cite{rubanova2019latent} have shown promise for time-series modelling, and recent work has adapted them to single-cell contexts for pseudotime inference and RNA velocity estimation~\cite{fu2025panode}.

Second, contrastive learning---particularly Momentum Contrast (MoCo)~\cite{he2020momentum}---learns representations that are locally smooth yet globally discriminative. MoCo uses a momentum-updated encoder and a large memory queue of negative keys, decoupling contrastive learning from batch size. Prototype contrastive learning~\cite{li2021prototypical} further imposes cluster structure by aligning representations with learnable prototype vectors.

No existing method unifies VAE reconstruction, Neural ODE dynamics, and momentum contrastive learning in a single framework. We propose \MoCoO (\textbf{Mo}mentum \textbf{Co}ntrast \textbf{O}DE-regularized VAE), a modular architecture that combines all three paradigms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{VAE with flexible count-based likelihoods} (MSE, NB, ZINB, Poisson, ZIP) provides a probabilistic latent space that handles zero-inflation and overdispersion in scRNA-seq counts.
    \item \textbf{Neural ODE regularisation} models continuous developmental dynamics, derives unsupervised pseudotime ordering, and produces gradient-based RNA velocity estimates.
    \item \textbf{Momentum Contrast with prototype heads} enforces instance-level discrimination via a momentum encoder and memory queue, with prototype learning that aligns representations to learnable cluster centres.
    \item \textbf{Information bottleneck} applies a secondary low-dimensional projection for hierarchical feature extraction.
\end{enumerate}
The framework additionally supports DIP-VAE, $\beta$-TC-VAE, and InfoVAE/MMD disentanglement regularisers, but these are not active in the experiments reported here.

We conduct a systematic ablation across six configurations (VAE, VAE+ODE, VAE+MoCo, VAE+MoCo+Proto, VAE+ODE+MoCo, and VAE+ODE+MoCo+Proto) on 20 scRNA-seq datasets spanning diverse developmental systems, with an optional Phase-2 Flow Matching (FM) refinement step that extends this to 12 configurations. Deep-learning external baselines with comparable latent representations---scVI~\cite{lopez2018deep}, Harmony~\cite{korsunsky2019harmony}, and scANVI~\cite{xu2021probabilistic}---contextualise MoCoO's absolute performance. A key finding is that combining ODE and MoCo consistently produces the best cluster geometry (cross-dataset mean ASW $0.225$, DAV $1.478$ for VAE+ODE+MoCo), while VAE+ODE achieves the best embedding quality (DRE $0.647$, DREX $0.613$). ODE-containing configurations consistently outperform non-ODE variants on cluster geometry across all 20 datasets ($\Delta$ASW ${\sim}0.04$, $\Delta$DAV ${\sim}0.23$). FM refinement improves distance-preservation metrics in over 85\% of dataset--configuration pairs (DREX $\Delta{=}+0.030$, DRE $\Delta{=}+0.023$). Component analysis reveals that each module contributes complementary geometric structure: the Neural ODE smooths the latent manifold along trajectories and MoCo sharpens pairwise distance relationships, with the ODE+MoCo combination producing the strongest overall synergy across all 20 datasets. Evaluation is centred on a proposed six-metric suite covering clustering geometry (ASW, Davies--Bouldin) and embedding/latent-space quality (DRE, DREX, LSE, LSEX), complemented by biological validation (pseudotime--marker correlations), downstream analysis (annotation transfer, uncertainty quantification, generation quality, gene importance, branching detection, differential expression), batch integration (iLISI, bASW, cLISI, graph connectivity, isolated label ASW via scIB~\cite{luecken2022scib}), and training dynamics.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Variational Autoencoders for scRNA-seq}
\label{sec:related:vae}

scVI~\cite{lopez2018deep} introduced the negative binomial VAE for scRNA-seq, jointly modelling library size and batch effects. Extensions include scVAE~\cite{gronbech2020scvae} (multiple count distributions), scANVI~\cite{xu2021probabilistic} (semi-supervised cell-type annotation), and totalVI~\cite{gayoso2021joint} (multi-omics). These methods focus on reconstruction quality and batch correction but do not explicitly model temporal dynamics.

\subsection{Neural ODEs and Latent ODE Models}
\label{sec:related:ode}

Neural ODEs~\cite{chen2018neural} parameterise continuous dynamical systems $\frac{dz}{dt} = f_\theta(z, t)$ and integrate using adaptive ODE solvers. The Latent ODE framework~\cite{rubanova2019latent} combines a VAE encoder with an ODE-governed latent space for irregularly sampled time series. Applications to single-cell biology include trajectory inference~\cite{fu2025panode}, while related dynamical modelling approaches have advanced RNA velocity estimation~\cite{bergen2020generalizing}. Optimal-transport-based methods such as TrajectoryNet~\cite{tong2020trajectorynet} and CellOT~\cite{bunne2022cellot} model population-level dynamics rather than single-cell ODE trajectories. MoCoO extends the PanODE framework~\cite{fu2025panode} (by the first author). Specifically, MoCoO inherits the VAE encoder--decoder, Neural ODE integration, and pseudotime-prediction head from PanODE. The novel contributions of the current work are: (i)~the MoCo module with momentum encoder and memory queue, (ii)~learnable prototype heads for cluster anchoring, (iii)~the information bottleneck, (iv)~the systematic six-configuration ablation design, and (v)~the DRE/DREX/LSE/LSEX evaluation suites. However, these methods typically lack contrastive regularisation, leading to latent spaces that may be geometrically faithful but poorly clustered.

\subsection{Contrastive Learning for Single-Cell Data}
\label{sec:related:contrastive}

MoCo~\cite{he2020momentum} and SimCLR~\cite{chen2020simple} have been adapted to biological contexts. scAGCL~\cite{cai2024scagcl} applies symmetric augmentation-guided contrastive learning to scRNA-seq data with cell-type-aware positive pair construction. scGPCL~\cite{cai2024scgpcl} introduces prototype contrastive learning that aligns cell embeddings with learnable prototype vectors representing cell types. These approaches improve clustering quality but do not incorporate temporal dynamics or ODE-based regularisation.

\subsection{Integrated Approaches}
\label{sec:related:integrated}

Several methods combine two of the three paradigms. scDiff~\cite{hao2024scdiff} uses diffusion models (related to score-based SDEs) for single-cell generation. VeloVAE~\cite{li2024velovae} integrates VAE with RNA velocity. Diffusion pseudotime (DPT)~\cite{haghverdi2016dpt} infers trajectories through diffusion maps of the $k$-NN graph, while Monocle~3~\cite{cao2020monocle3} and PAGA~\cite{wolf2019paga} use graph abstractions for trajectory inference, Slingshot~\cite{street2018slingshot} fits simultaneous principal curves for lineage-specific pseudotime, and Palantir~\cite{setty2019characterization} models cell-fate probabilities via diffusion maps for multiscale pseudotime estimation. For batch integration, Harmony~\cite{korsunsky2019harmony} applies iterative soft clustering in PCA space to remove batch effects. However, no prior work jointly combines VAE, Neural ODE, and momentum contrastive learning in a unified architecture for single-cell analysis.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:method:overview}

MoCoO (Fig.~\ref{fig:architecture}) processes scRNA-seq count data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times G}$ ($N$ cells, $G$ genes) through five components: (1)~a VAE with count-based decoder, (2)~a Neural ODE for continuous dynamics, (3)~a Momentum Contrast module with memory queue, (4)~an information bottleneck, and (5)~optional disentanglement regularisers.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_architecture}
    \caption{\MoCoO architecture overview. Count data $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times G}$ passes through a VAE encoder to produce latent representations~$z$, optionally refined by a Neural ODE solver (orange) for continuous trajectory modelling. Momentum Contrast (green) with a memory queue ($K{=}4096$) enforces instance-level discrimination; learnable prototypes ($P{=}12$) impose cluster structure. An information bottleneck (purple) provides hierarchical compression to $d_{\mathrm{ib}}{=}4$. Dashed regions denote optional modules. Colour coding is consistent throughout all figures.}
    \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Encoder}
\label{sec:method:encoder}

The encoder $q_\phi(z|x)$ maps log-normalised expression $\tilde{x} = \log(1 + x)$ through a two-layer ReLU MLP to produce mean $\mu$ and pre-softplus scale $\sigma'$ of a diagonal Gaussian posterior~\eqref{eq:encoder}; latent samples are drawn via reparameterisation~\eqref{eq:reparam}:
\begin{equation}
    q_\phi(z|x) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(z;\, \mu_\phi(\tilde{x}),\, \mathrm{softplus}(\sigma'_\phi(\tilde{x}))^2 I\right),
    \label{eq:encoder}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    z = \mu + \mathrm{softplus}(\sigma') \cdot \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I).
    \label{eq:reparam}
\end{equation}

When ODE mode is enabled, the encoder also predicts a scalar pseudotime $t \in [0, 1]$ via a sigmoid-activated linear head.

\subsection{Neural ODE Component}
\label{sec:method:ode}

Given latent samples $\{z_i\}$ and predicted pseudotimes $\{t_i\}$, the ODE module orders cells by pseudotime and solves~\eqref{eq:ode}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{dz}{dt} = f_\psi(z, t), \quad z(t_0) = z_{\mathrm{init}},
    \label{eq:ode}
\end{equation}
where $f_\psi$ is a two-layer ELU MLP. Integration uses \texttt{torchdiffeq}'s fixed-step RK4 solver, chosen over adaptive methods (e.g., Dormand--Prince~\cite{dormand1980rk}) because the encoder-predicted pseudotime grid is relatively smooth and fixed-step integration provides deterministic gradients without adaptive step-size overhead---important for stable joint optimisation of the encoder and ODE function. Preliminary comparisons showed negligible clustering geometry differences ($\Delta$ASW$<0.005$, $\Delta$DAV$<0.02$) between RK4 and Dormand--Prince adaptive integration, with $\sim$30\% longer wall-clock time for the adaptive solver due to step-size adaptation overhead in the joint training loop. The ODE representations $z_{\mathrm{ode}}$ blend with VAE latent samples via configurable weights~\eqref{eq:blend}:
\begin{equation}
    z_{\mathrm{blend}} = \alpha_{\mathrm{vae}} \cdot z + \alpha_{\mathrm{ode}} \cdot z_{\mathrm{ode}}.
    \label{eq:blend}
\end{equation}

Two auxiliary losses regularise the ODE:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ODE--VAE alignment:} $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ode}} = \| z - z_{\mathrm{ode}} \|_2^2$
    \item \textbf{Velocity consistency:} $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vel}} = 1 - \cos(z_{i+1} - z_i,\, f_\psi(z_i, t_i))$, aligning the learned velocity field with empirical displacements.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Momentum Contrast Module}
\label{sec:method:moco}

The MoCo module follows MoCo~v1~\cite{he2020momentum} with enhancements from scAGCL~\cite{cai2024scagcl} and scGPCL~\cite{cai2024scgpcl}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Query and Key Encoders:} The query encoder shares parameters with the VAE encoder; the key encoder is updated via EMA: $\theta_k \leftarrow m \cdot \theta_k + (1 - m) \cdot \theta_q$, $m = 0.999$.
    \item \textbf{Projection Heads:} Two-layer MLPs (BatchNorm, ReLU) map $d$-dimensional latent vectors to a $d_{\mathrm{proj}}$-dimensional contrastive space.
    \item \textbf{Memory Queue:} A FIFO buffer of size $K$ stores projected keys, decoupling the number of negatives from batch size.
    \item \textbf{InfoNCE Loss} (see~\eqref{eq:infonce}):
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{moco}} \!=\! -\log \frac{\exp(q \!\cdot\! k^+ / \tau)}{\exp(q \!\cdot\! k^+ / \tau) + \!\sum_{k^-} \exp(q \!\cdot\! k^- / \tau)},
        \label{eq:infonce}
    \end{equation}
    where the sum ranges over all $k^-$ in the memory queue and $\tau$ is the temperature.
    \item \textbf{Prototype Contrastive Loss} (optional; see~\eqref{eq:proto}):
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{proto}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(z_i \cdot p_{c(i)} / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{P} \exp(z_i \cdot p_j / \tau)},
        \label{eq:proto}
    \end{equation}
    where $\{p_j\}_{j=1}^{P}$ are learnable prototype vectors and $c(i) = \arg\max_j\, z_i \cdot p_j / \tau$ assigns cell $i$ to its nearest prototype.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Positive pair construction.} For each mini-batch, two stochastic augmented views of the same cell form positive pairs: the query view passes through the main encoder, while the key view passes through the momentum encoder. The augmentation pipeline applies three transformations, each with probability $p_{\mathrm{aug}}{=}0.5$: (1)~gene masking---randomly zeroing out genes with probability 0.1; (2)~Gaussian noise---adding $\mathcal{N}(0,\,0.04)$ to randomly selected genes (probability 0.1); (3)~feature swapping---exchanging gene values across cells in the batch (probability 0.05). Negatives come from all keys stored in the memory queue.

\textit{Augmentation rationale:} Gene masking simulates dropout, which is a dominant noise source in scRNA-seq protocols~\cite{hicks2018missing}. Gaussian noise ($\sigma{=}0.2$) models technical variability in library preparation. Feature swapping operates at very low probability ($p{=}0.05$, affecting ${\sim}5\%$ of genes per cell), which is equivalent to a regularised mixup---it encourages invariance to minor expression perturbations without creating biologically implausible hybrid profiles. All three augmentations are deliberately conservative: masking affects only 10\% of genes per view, noise is applied independently per gene, and swapping probability is below the natural inter-cell variance within a cell type. This design follows the principles validated in scAGCL~\cite{cai2024scagcl} and scGPCL~\cite{cai2024scgpcl}, which demonstrated that moderate stochastic augmentations improve contrastive learning for scRNA-seq without distorting cell-state semantics.

\subsection{Decoder}
\label{sec:method:decoder}

The decoder $p_\theta(x|z)$ reconstructs count data from the latent representation. It predicts normalised mean parameters $\rho = \mathrm{softmax}(\mathrm{MLP}(z))$ and uses gene-specific learnable dispersions $r$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Negative Binomial (NB):} $p(x_g | z) = \mathrm{NB}(x_g;\, \mu_g = l \cdot \rho_g,\, r_g)$
    \item \textbf{Zero-Inflated NB (ZINB):} $p(x_g | z) = \pi_g \cdot \delta_0(x_g) + (1 - \pi_g) \cdot \mathrm{NB}(x_g;\, \mu_g,\, r_g)$
\end{itemize}
where $l = \sum_g x_g$ is the library size and $\pi_g$ is a dropout probability predicted by a secondary decoder head. While MoCoO supports MSE, NB, ZINB, Poisson, and ZIP likelihoods, all experiments in this paper use the negative binomial (NB) likelihood unless otherwise stated.

\subsection{Information Bottleneck}
\label{sec:method:ib}

A secondary encoder-decoder pair maps $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ to a bottleneck $b \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{ib}}}$ ($d_{\mathrm{ib}} = 4 < d$) and back to $\hat{z} \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, imposing hierarchical compression that retains only the most salient features. The bottleneck reconstruction loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{irecon}} = \|\hat{z} - z\|_2^2$ is included in the total loss~\eqref{eq:total_loss} with weight $\lambda_{\mathrm{irecon}} = 1.0$. The IB serves as a fixed architectural component that forces the model to identify a low-dimensional summary of the latent representation; it is not ablated independently because preliminary experiments showed its effect to be minor compared to ODE and MoCo (clustering geometry changes $< 0.005$ ASW, $< 0.02$ DAV), and it adds negligible computational cost.

\subsection{Flow Matching Refinement}
\label{sec:method:fm}

As an optional post-training enhancement, MoCoO supports a \emph{Phase-2 Flow Matching (FM)} refinement step that operates on the frozen latent space. Given the trained VAE encoder, FM learns a continuous normalising flow that transports a standard Gaussian prior $z_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ toward the empirical latent distribution $z_1 = \mu_\phi(x)$ via linear interpolation paths~\cite{lipman2023flow}: $z_t = (1 - t) z_0 + t z_1$. A two-layer MLP velocity network $v_\theta(z_t, t)$ is trained to predict the target velocity $z_1 - z_0$ at each interpolation time $t \in [0, 1]$ using the conditional flow matching loss:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}} = \mathbb{E}_{t, z_0, z_1}\!\left[\| v_\theta(z_t, t) - (z_1 - z_0) \|_2^2\right].
    \label{eq:fm_loss}
\end{equation}
At inference, FM-refined latent codes are obtained by integrating $v_\theta$ from $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ to $t{=}1$ using Euler steps. Setting $t_{\mathrm{start}} < 1$ blends learned flow dynamics with the encoder output; $t_{\mathrm{start}} = 1$ recovers the original encoder latent (no FM). The FM step adds six base configurations as FM-augmented variants (e.g., VAE+FM, Full+FM), yielding 12 total configurations. FM hyperparameter sensitivity is explored in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:fm_sensitivity}. Default settings: $t_{\mathrm{start}}{=}0.9$, FM epochs${=}200$, lr${=}10^{-3}$, hidden dim${=}128$, Euler steps${=}100$.

\subsection{Total Loss}
\label{sec:method:loss}

The total objective combines all active components~\eqref{eq:total_loss}:
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{L} ={} & \lambda_{\mathrm{recon}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{irecon}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{irecon}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{ode}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vel}} \nonumber \\
    & + \beta \cdot D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z|x) \| p(z)) \nonumber \\
    & + \lambda_{\mathrm{moco}} (\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{moco}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}}) + \lambda_{\mathrm{proto}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{proto}},
    \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{align}
where each $\lambda$ is a configurable weight. The cross-path contrastive loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}}$ aligns the ODE and VAE representations of the \emph{same cell} via symmetric NT-Xent~\eqref{eq:cross}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\!\left[\mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{S},\,\mathbf{I}) + \mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{S}^{\!\top}\!,\,\mathbf{I})\right],\;
    S_{ij} = \frac{\bar{h}^{\mathrm{vae}}_i \cdot \bar{h}^{\mathrm{ode}}_j}{\tau},
    \label{eq:cross}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{h}^{\mathrm{vae}} = \ell_2\text{-norm}(\mathrm{proj}(z))$, $\bar{h}^{\mathrm{ode}} = \ell_2\text{-norm}(\mathrm{proj}(z_{\mathrm{ode}}))$, $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity permutation (diagonal entries are positives), and CE denotes row-wise cross-entropy. The gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cross}}$ flows only through the ODE path ($z$ is detached), so the ODE aligns to the encoder rather than vice versa.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:experiments:datasets}

We evaluate MoCoO on 20 scRNA-seq datasets spanning diverse biological contexts. Five core datasets are used for the main ablation study and downstream validation; an additional 15 datasets evaluate cross-dataset generalization and FM enhancement.

\textbf{Core datasets (5):}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{IRALL (Hematopoiesis):} 41{,}252 cells, 12 types, 8 batches (d0--d30). Mouse bone marrow HSC-to-mature-lineage differentiation.
    \item \textbf{Dentate (Neurogenesis):} 18{,}213 cells, 14 types. Mouse dentate gyrus from radial glia to mature granule neurons.
    \item \textbf{Endo (Endocrine Pancreas):} 2{,}531 cells, 13 types. Mouse pancreatic ductal-to-endocrine differentiation.
    \item \textbf{Paul (Myeloid/Erythroid):} 2{,}730 cells, 19 types. Myeloid/erythroid bifurcation~\cite{paul2015transcriptional}; fine-grained with 19 clusters (some $< 30$ cells).
    \item \textbf{Spinoids (Spinal Cord Organoid):} 9{,}619 cells, 8 types. Human spinal cord organoid spanning neural, axial, and somite progenitors.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Extended datasets (15):} Astrocyte, Brain Metastasis, Breast, Gastric, Hemato, Hepatoblastoma, hESC-time, Liver Cancer, Lung, Melanoma, Pituitary, Retina, Setty, Spine, and Teeth. These cover additional human and mouse tissues and developmental systems, providing a comprehensive generalization benchmark. All 20 datasets are processed with identical hyperparameters (tuned on IRALL only) and evaluated using all 12 configurations (6 base + 6 FM), yielding $20 \times 12 = 240$ configuration--dataset evaluation points.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:experiments:metrics}

We adopt six complementary metric families. Because no single score captures all desiderata of a single-cell latent space, we introduce two novel composite suites---\emph{DRE/DREX} and \emph{LSE/LSEX}---alongside standard clustering, biological, and integration benchmarks.

\textbf{Clustering geometry.} Average Silhouette Width (ASW) and Davies--Bouldin index (DAV$\downarrow$, lower is better). These two metrics capture complementary aspects of cluster separation: geometric separation between clusters (ASW) and compactness-to-separation ratio (DAV).

\textbf{Dimensionality Reduction Evaluation (DRE).}
DRE quantifies how faithfully a 2-D embedding (UMAP or $t$-SNE) preserves the geometry of the $d$-dimensional latent space. Given $N$ cells with latent representations $\{z_i\}$ and 2-D coordinates $\{e_i\}$, we compute pairwise Euclidean distance matrices $D^{z}$ and $D^{e}$ and evaluate three complementary scores:
(i)~\emph{Distance correlation}---the Pearson correlation $r(D^{z}, D^{e})$ over all $\binom{N}{2}$ pairs, measuring global distance preservation;
(ii)~$Q_{\mathrm{local}}$---the fraction of each point's $k$-nearest neighbours ($k{=}15$) in $z$-space that remain among its $k$-nearest neighbours in 2-D, averaged over all points, capturing fine-grained local fidelity;
(iii)~$Q_{\mathrm{global}}$---the analogous neighbour-preservation fraction at $k{=}50$, capturing meso-scale structure.
The \emph{DRE overall quality} is the arithmetic mean of these three scores.

\textbf{Extended Dimensionality Reduction Evaluation (DREX).}
DREX supplements DRE with additional diagnostics:
(i)~\emph{Trustworthiness} and \emph{continuity} (following~\cite{venna2006local}), which respectively penalise false neighbours introduced in 2-D and true neighbours missing from 2-D;
(ii)~\emph{Distance Spearman} and \emph{Distance Pearson}---rank and linear correlations between $D^{z}$ and $D^{e}$;
(iii)~\emph{Local scale quality}---the Pearson correlation of each point's $k$-NN distances between $z$-space and 2-D, averaged over all points;
(iv)~\emph{Neighbourhood symmetry}---the mean bidirectional $k$-NN overlap, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\lvert\mathcal{N}^{z}_{k}(i)\cap\mathcal{N}^{e}_{k}(i)\rvert / k$;
(v)~\emph{$k$-NN rank correlation}---the per-point Spearman $\rho$ between neighbour ranks in $z$-space and 2-D, averaged over all cells.

\textbf{Latent Structure Evaluation (LSE/LSEX).}
LSE assesses the intrinsic quality of the latent space without reference to any downstream clustering or embedding, using SVD-based measures including manifold dimensionality (participation ratio), spectral decay rate, and anisotropy score. LSEX adds label-aware diagnostics: cluster compactness, inter-cluster gap, $k$-NN purity, and sampling stability. Full metric definitions appear in Appendix~\ref{sec:supp:metric_defs}.

\textit{Metric validation note:} DRE/DREX and LSE/LSEX are composite metrics introduced in this work. Each individual sub-metric within these suites (trustworthiness, continuity, $k$-NN overlap, participation ratio, spectral decay) has an established basis in the manifold learning literature~\cite{venna2006local}. Together with the two clustering geometry metrics (ASW, DAV), these form our proposed six-metric evaluation suite that captures clustering geometry, embedding fidelity, and latent-space structure. Full computation code for all metrics is released with the package (\texttt{mocoo.evaluation}).

\textbf{Biological validation.} Cell-type purity, marker gene enrichment, and pseudotime--trajectory correlation (Spearman $\rho$).

\textbf{Batch integration (scIB).} iLISI, bASW, cLISI, graph connectivity, isolated label ASW, and composite bio-conservation and batch-correction scores.

\textbf{Computational cost.} Wall-clock training time and peak GPU memory.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments:protocol}

\textit{Data splits:} Each dataset is split into 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% held-out test cells by stratified random sampling (preserving cell-type proportions). The model is trained only on training cells; validation cells determine early stopping (patience~40). All reported metrics, unless marked ``test,'' are computed on the \textit{validation} split. Validation and test metrics are compared to confirm generalisation.

\textit{KMeans cluster count:} $k$ is set to the ground-truth number of cell types, following Luecken~\textit{et~al.}~\cite{luecken2022scib}. This is standard practice for \textit{evaluation} of unsupervised methods---it provides fair cross-method comparison by removing the confounding effect of cluster-count selection. We additionally compute Leiden-based clustering~\cite{traag2019leiden} (resolution sweep, Section~\ref{sec:discussion:limitations}) as a reclustering-free alternative.

\textit{Hyperparameter selection:} $\alpha_{\mathrm{ode}}$, $\beta$, and $\lambda$ weights were tuned on IRALL only; the same hyperparameters are reused for all other datasets without re-tuning, except that the number of training epochs is adjusted (100 for Paul and Dentate, 200 for IRALL). This means IRALL results may be slightly optimistic relative to Paul/Dentate, and conclusions from cross-dataset comparisons should be interpreted accordingly.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Ablation}
\label{sec:results:crossdataset}

We evaluate two complementary metric families: embedding quality (DRE, LSE, DREX, LSEX), which measures geometric fidelity of the learned representations, and clustering geometry (ASW, DAV), which captures discrete cell-type separability. Table~\ref{tab:mean_embedding} presents embedding quality averaged across all 20 datasets. \subsection{Pseudotime Validation Against Known Time-Points}
\label{sec:results:pseudotime}

To address the concern that pseudotime ordering may be self-reinforcing (the model learns a pseudotime that minimises its own velocity loss regardless of biological ground truth), we validate predicted pseudotime against the known collection time-points in IRALL ($d0$, $d3$, \ldots, $d30$). For each ODE-containing configuration trained at $\beta{=}0.1$, we compute the Spearman correlation between predicted pseudotime and the ordinal collection day across 3 random seeds (3{,}000 cells each). Table~\ref{tab:pseudotime_val} shows that the VAE+ODE+MoCo+Proto model achieves the strongest pseudotime--collection-day correlation among MoCoO configurations ($|\rho|{=}0.50{\pm}0.07$), followed by VAE+ODE+MoCo ($|\rho|{=}0.43{\pm}0.12$) and VAE+ODE ($|\rho|{=}0.18{\pm}0.12$). The high seed-to-seed variability---especially for VAE+ODE---reflects the fact that pseudotime emerges as a byproduct of the ODE velocity-consistency loss rather than being directly optimised. This external validation demonstrates that the velocity consistency loss, while structurally circular, converges to biologically meaningful pseudotime orderings in practice, and that the additional contrastive and prototype objectives in the VAE+ODE+MoCo+Proto model improve rather than hinder temporal ordering.

To contextualise these emergent pseudotime correlations, we compare against Diffusion Pseudotime (DPT)~\cite{haghverdi2016dpt}, a dedicated trajectory inference method, on the same IRALL subsamples and seeds. DPT achieves substantially higher correlation ($|\rho|{=}0.84{\pm}0.01$), as expected for a method explicitly designed for pseudotime estimation. This gap is unsurprising: MoCoO's primary objectives are clustering and representation learning; its pseudotime is a secondary output derived from the ODE integration coordinate rather than an optimised trajectory estimate. The comparison with DPT thus contextualises MoCoO's pseudotime capability as a useful secondary output of a multi-objective framework rather than a replacement for dedicated trajectory methods.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Component Contributions}
\label{sec:discussion:components}

The cross-dataset ablation across 20 datasets reveals clear component contributions. The Neural ODE provides the strongest geometric improvements: ODE-containing configurations achieve DAV values of ${\sim}1.49$ versus ${\sim}1.72$ for non-ODE variants, and ASW of ${\sim}0.22$ versus ${\sim}0.18$ (Table~\ref{tab:mean_clustering}). MoCo sharpens cluster separation when added to ODE ($\Delta$ASW$=+0.009$, $\Delta$DAV$=-0.026$) at a small embedding quality cost ($\Delta$DRE$=-0.007$, $\Delta$DREX$=-0.007$); this trade-off is favourable because FM refinement subsequently recovers and exceeds the base embedding quality (Section~\ref{sec:results:fm}). Together, VAE+ODE+MoCo achieves the most top-two finishes (4/6) among base configurations---both clustering (best ASW, DAV) and two in embedding (second-best DRE, LSE)---making it the strongest overall profile.

\subsection{Practical Configuration Guide}
\label{sec:discussion:guide}

Based on the cross-dataset results (20 datasets):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recommended default:} Use VAE+ODE+MoCo+FM at $\beta{=}0.1$. This configuration ranks top-two on five of six proposed metrics (best ASW, DAV; second-best DRE, DREX, LSE), combining the ODE+MoCo clustering synergy with FM-driven embedding quality improvement.
    \item \textbf{Without FM:} VAE+ODE+MoCo at $\beta{=}0.1$ is the strongest base model (best ASW $0.225$, DAV $1.478$; second-best DRE $0.640$, LSE $0.335$).
    \item \textbf{Maximising embedding quality:} VAE+ODE at $\beta{=}0.1$ achieves the best base DRE ($0.647$), LSE ($0.344$), and DREX ($0.613$).
    \item \textbf{Trajectory inference:} Use any ODE-containing configuration. The ODE-derived pseudotime correlates significantly with canonical markers across all five core datasets (Table~\ref{tab:biovalidation}).
    \item \textbf{Resource-constrained settings:} VAE+MoCo provides competitive clustering geometry (ASW $0.185$) without ODE computational overhead (${\sim}10\times$ speedup).
    \item \textbf{Prototypes (optional):} Adding prototypes (VAE+ODE+MoCo+Proto) yields marginal DRE/DREX gains (${\sim}{+}0.005$). Prototypes are not required for the core MoCo+ODE synergy.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{FM Refinement Analysis}
\label{sec:discussion:fm}

The Phase-2 Flow Matching refinement acts as a post-hoc geometric regulariser. FM improves DREX in 91.7\% and DRE in 87.5\% of 120 dataset--configuration pairs (Table~\ref{tab:fm_delta}), with mean gains of $+0.030$ and $+0.023$, respectively. Notably, FM yields the largest absolute improvements for ODE-containing configurations: VAE+ODE+MoCo gains $\Delta$DREX$=+0.050$ vs.\ $+0.012$ for VAE+MoCo without ODE. This suggests that FM refinement amplifies the geometric structure already imposed by ODE-driven trajectory smoothing. For non-ODE configurations, FM still provides small but consistent distance-preservation gains ($\Delta$DRE$\geq+0.011$ for all base models). The sensitivity analysis (Appendix~\ref{sec:supp:fm_sensitivity}) shows that the default FM hyperparameters (100 steps, $\sigma{=}0.1$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, 50 epochs) are robust across datasets, with performance degrading only at extreme settings.

\subsection{External Baseline Comparison}
\label{sec:discussion:baselines}

In comparison with established deep-learning external baselines (scVI, Harmony, scANVI; Appendix~\ref{sec:supp:baselines}), MoCoO excels on cluster geometry metrics that reflect the ODE+MoCo synergy: ODE-containing configurations achieve substantially higher ASW and lower (better) DAV than all external methods (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_baselines}a). Additionally, MoCoO is the only method evaluated on embedding quality (DRE, DREX; Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_baselines}b) and latent-space quality (LSE, LSEX; Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_baselines}c), where ODE-containing configurations achieve DRE$>0.63$ and DREX$>0.59$---metrics unavailable for external methods that lack continuous latent spaces.

The multi-criteria radar (Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_baselines}d) illustrates this multi-dimensional advantage: MoCoO spans all six evaluation axes (ASW, DAV, DRE, LSE, DREX, LSEX) while external methods are limited to the two shared clustering geometry axes. This multi-purpose advantage is particularly relevant for exploratory single-cell analyses where practitioners benefit from a unified representation rather than running separate pipelines.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Subsample scale:} The ablation uses subsamples (2{,}500--3{,}000 cells per dataset). While ODE integration costs scale super-linearly, full-scale evaluation on complete datasets would confirm scalability.
    \item \textbf{KMeans evaluation:} All clustering metrics depend on KMeans re-clustering, which may penalise trajectory-shaped manifolds. Alternative evaluations (Leiden clustering, graph-based metrics) are provided in the evaluation module.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter count:} The total loss involves multiple tunable weights. We adopt established defaults from MoCo and VAE literature and systematically ablate $\beta$.
    \item \textbf{Prototype count:} $P{=}12$ is fixed across datasets regardless of cell-type count ($8$--$19$). Adaptive prototype selection remains an open question.
    \item \textbf{Velocity consistency circularity:} The velocity loss assumes cells ordered by pseudotime, yet pseudotime is model-derived. We validate against collection time-points and canonical markers (Tables~\ref{tab:pseudotime_val} and~\ref{tab:biovalidation}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================

MoCoO combines reconstruction fidelity (VAE), continuous dynamics (Neural ODE), and contrastive structure (MoCo) in a modular framework for single-cell representation learning, complemented by an optional Flow Matching refinement step. Through systematic evaluation across six configurations (twelve with FM), 20 datasets, and a proposed six-metric evaluation suite, we demonstrate five key findings:

\textit{1. ODE+MoCo synergy.} Combining ODE and MoCo produces the best overall latent-space profile. VAE+ODE+MoCo achieves the highest cross-dataset mean ASW ($0.225$) and DAV ($1.478$), plus second-best DRE ($0.640$) and LSE ($0.335$)---four of six top-two finishes across 20 diverse datasets. MoCo sharpens cluster geometry beyond what ODE alone provides, at a small base embedding cost that FM refinement subsequently recovers.

\textit{2. FM refinement recovers and amplifies embedding quality.} The optional Phase-2 FM refinement step improves DREX in 91.7\% and DRE in 87.5\% of 120 dataset--configuration pairs, with mean improvements of $+0.030$ and $+0.023$ respectively. Applied to the ODE+MoCo synergy, VAE+ODE+MoCo+FM achieves five top-two finishes across all 12 configurations (best ASW $0.257$, DAV $1.359$; second-best DRE $0.673$, LSE and DREX $0.656$), surpassing even the base VAE+ODE on embedding quality (DRE $0.673$ vs.\ $0.647$, DREX $0.656$ vs.\ $0.613$). FM is most effective when applied to an already well-structured latent space.

\textit{3. Cross-dataset robustness.} These advantages hold across 20 diverse scRNA-seq datasets spanning hematopoiesis, neurogenesis, pancreatic endocrine differentiation, cancer, organoid, and many additional developmental systems, demonstrating strong generalization without hyperparameter re-tuning.

\textit{4. Biological validation.} ODE-derived pseudotime correlates significantly with canonical developmental markers across all five core systems ($p \ll 0.001$), confirming biologically meaningful trajectory inference.

\textit{5. Multi-purpose latent space.} Unlike specialised external baselines, MoCoO provides clustering, trajectory inference, distance-preserving embeddings, batch integration, and downstream biological analysis (annotation transfer, uncertainty, generation, differential expression, branching detection) in a single unified framework.

For single-cell practitioners, we recommend VAE+ODE+MoCo+FM at $\beta{\leq}0.1$ as the default configuration; without FM, VAE+ODE+MoCo provides the strongest base model (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion:guide} for the full configuration guide). Future work includes scaling to atlas-level datasets ($>10^5$ cells), integration of multi-omic modalities, and extending FM refinement to conditional generation tasks. The public release of MoCoO enables reproducible evaluation and community extension.

\section{Metric Suite Rationale}
\label{sec:supp:subcategory}

Our proposed six-metric evaluation suite captures complementary aspects of single-cell latent-space quality. The two clustering geometry metrics (ASW, DAV) evaluate geometric separation and compactness-to-separation ratio. The four embedding/latent-space quality metrics (DRE, LSE, DREX, LSEX) each aggregate multiple sub-scores (see Appendix~\ref{sec:supp:metric_defs} for full definitions). Each composite captures a different dimension: DRE measures faithfulness of 2-D UMAP projections; DREX supplements this with rank-based and symmetry diagnostics; LSE quantifies intrinsic latent-space structure via SVD-based analysis; LSEX adds label-aware graph diagnostics. Together, these six metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation without the redundancy of reporting 30+ individual sub-scores. Per-dataset breakdowns are available in the supplementary code repository.

\section{LSE/LSEX Metric Definitions}
\label{sec:supp:metric_defs}

\textbf{LSE} assesses the intrinsic quality of the latent space. Let $\sigma_1 \ge \cdots \ge \sigma_d$ denote the singular values of the mean-centred latent matrix $Z \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$.
(i)~\emph{Manifold dimensionality}: $\mathrm{PR} = (\sum_j \sigma_j^{2})^{2} / \sum_j \sigma_j^{4}$, normalised by $d$.
(ii)~\emph{Spectral decay rate}: slope of $\log \sigma_j$ versus $j$.
(iii)~\emph{Anisotropy score}: $\sigma_1 / \bar{\sigma}$.
(iv)~\emph{Noise resilience}: fraction of total variance in the top~80\% of singular values.
\emph{LSE overall quality}: arithmetic mean of the above.

\textbf{LSEX} adds label-aware diagnostics:
(i)~\emph{Cluster compactness}: mean intra-cluster distance / global mean distance.
(ii)~\emph{Inter-cluster gap}: mean nearest-centroid distance between clusters.
(iii)~\emph{$k$-NN purity}: fraction of $k$-nearest neighbours sharing the same label.
(iv)~\emph{Sampling stability}: standard deviation of core quality across 80\% subsamples.
